The roadbook is the quietest piece of technology in rallying: paper, ink, and a convention
old enough to have grandchildren. It is also the reason two people in a loud car can cross
a subcontinent without an argument. Mostly.

## The tulip

Each instruction is a small diagram — a "tulip" — with a ball for where you come from and an
arrow for where you're going. Between tulips, a distance. That's the whole language. Like
all good notation, it holds exactly as much information as you can process at speed and not
one stroke more.

## Distance is the co-driver's heartbeat

The trip meter is the co-driver's instrument, and the rhythm of a good crew is calls made
by distance, confirmed by landscape: *eight hundred… four hundred… junction, right, onto
the bigger road.* When the numbers and the world agree, the car flows. When they disagree,
a good co-driver says so early and without drama.

## Trust is the actual technology

A driver flat over a crest is acting on a sentence spoken by someone reading a page. That's
the sport, reduced to its essence: preparation converted into trust, at speed. Everything we
do in a programme — the shakedown week, the pace-note practice, the co-driver pairing — is
really the manufacture of that trust before it gets tested.

## Why our site looks the way it does

Regulars will have noticed the column down the left edge of these pages behaves like a
roadbook: tulips, distances, a finish control. That isn't decoration. It's the grammar this
team thinks in, applied to the one stage we run on the internet.
